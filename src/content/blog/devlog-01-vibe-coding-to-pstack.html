This is the first Prjkt Studios dev log. Prjkt Studios is what I call the little collection of apps I build for myself: Wherewithal, Everglae and Valkyr so far, with Aufstieg and Anthem still cooking. These logs are where I write about building them.

So this whole thing started about a month and a half ago. I had been using Claude Code for my academic and work related stuff, but I started noticing that I never hit 100% of my weekly usage, and honestly it felt like I was wasting a big opportunity.

Coincidentally, I had just fixed an old laptop of mine, and a friend told me about Remote Control in Claude Code. That was kind of a game changer. Now the old laptop sits plugged in at a corner of the room, and I just give it tasks while I sleep, go out or work on other stuff. So I thought this was my time to actually start building something and get my money's worth out of Claude Code.

Everglae, or how to rickroll yourself

I have always had ideas for apps I would personally want on my phone. One of them was an app locker that plays a video when an app locks, so once my time runs out I either get rickrolled or get some dumb motivational video telling me to stop doomscrolling and do something actually useful.

The app sounded pretty straightforward, so I just got Claude to cook something up. Luckily I had done Android development on that old laptop before, so it already had all the tools it needed. I gave Claude my ideas and told it to make an Expo app. I admit my prompting at the start was very basic, and it took a fair bit of directing and micromanaging to get it to do exactly what I wanted. I plugged in my Pixel so it could test the app on a real phone and keep building overnight while I slept.

I started on a Thursday afternoon, and by that night I had a base version with daily limits, timed locks, a strict mode and onboarding. Under the hood it uses an accessibility service to notice which app is in front, Android's usage stats to add up how long I've spent in it, and a full screen activity that plays the video.

I also wanted it to have a distinct UI style and a bit of personality, so I made a small mascot for it and got Claude to bring it to life. Fun fact: Claude wrote all ten of the mascot's animations (idle, hop, spin, yawn, sneeze and so on) as raw Lottie JSON, using Python scripts. No After Effects involved. I named it Everglae because I had Pretty Patterns on loop those days and wanted to give a nod to it.

Wherewithal, the notebook I never kept up with

In my view Everglae was a great success. So I remembered another app I had wanted to make for a long time: an app to track my finances. Yes, I was one of those guys who had a notebook and wrote down how much I spent. But the notebook got forgotten after a while, I stopped keeping up with my finances, and I realised I was spending a lot.

Coincidentally (again), I came across Matt Pocock and his skills for Claude. I hadn't really used many Claude skills at this point (yes, I was a complete noob), so I went through them and found a few that looked interesting. /grill-me was one of them, so I got it into a local repo and started using it to work out my ideas with Claude. I gotta say I loved it. I felt like I could articulate what I wanted much better, and Claude and I ended up with a proper shared understanding of the app before anything got built.

And so Wherewithal was born. I started it on the Saturday after starting Everglae, and version 1.0 was running on my Pixel by Sunday night. It reads my bank's SMS messages and turns them into transactions. I named it Wherewithal as a nod to Lowswimmer, another artist I had on repeat. And I gotta say, Wherewithal might be my favourite app I've built, even now, after dabbling in a few more since.

Living with my own apps

What started as a way to use up my Claude usage became a very productive thing. The next few weeks were basically me using the apps and slowly iterating on them. You can check the release notes on wherewithal.prjktstudios.com and everglae.prjktstudios.com to see how they changed over the weeks. Wherewithal is on its twentieth release now.

I gotta admit that Wherewithal, although it's my favourite, was depressing to look at, because I could finally see how much I was actually spending.

During that time I thought these were good apps for me, but I could tell I only used them because I was the one who made them. I had doubts that anyone else would have the same enthusiasm, so I wasn't very keen to release them. Especially Wherewithal. It could read my bank texts, but cash and anything the bank didn't text me about I still had to add by hand, and sorting out categories took a while. I also found out that KiwiMoney is a thing, and it does something pretty close. And the same friend who got me onto Remote Control told me a coworker of his had built his own version of it too, so I knew I wasn't doing anything very novel. So I just kept iterating, making the apps look nice to me and adding all the features I wanted so I could enjoy using them myself.

Okay fine, the Play Store

The decision to maybe put them out came after a couple of my hometown friends, who aren't in the tech industry, saw the apps and got interested. I started sending them APKs, but some of them couldn't install them because Google Play Protect kept getting in the way. So I finally said screw it, got a Google Play developer account last week and set up testing tracks so they could install them from the Play Store.

And that's how we got here, in a nutshell. I have about three more apps I haven't talked about here, and if they turn out okay I might push them out too. Wherewithal has a special place for me, so I'm planning to promote it a bit and hopefully get it to a proper Play Store release, just for fun.

The part that didn't sit right

Btw, this whole post was meant to be about something else. As you probably guessed, these apps were pretty much vibe coded, and that never sat right with me. I barely wrote any code for them, and at the time I kind of hated that. I knew what the apps did and roughly how they worked, but I have always written code and been very much engaged with the codebase, so this felt weird.

Around this time I came across Matt Pocock and Lauren Tan (better known as Poteto), and listened to a podcast they did together. I got really interested in how they approach building products in this age of AI, and especially in something they talked about called a trust ladder.

The way I understood it: every time an agent gets something wrong, you push the fix as far up the ladder as it will go. The best fix is code that makes the mistake impossible. If you can't do that, a lint rule or a type check. If not that, a rule or a skill. And only at the bottom, someone reviewing it. On top of all that, you want a way for the agent to prove its own work, and you earn that trust before you let many agents loose in parallel.

The trust ladder, as I understand it · and where Wherewithal is has it partly not yet not sure

    Coding agents are already better developers than I am, so if I tried to put myself in the middle of the process, I would just become the bottleneck. Maybe what I should be striving for instead is making sure the agents actually do what I want, and that they work in the best, most controlled environment possible, so the apps come out safe and sound. That's how I got hooked on the "good codebase and verification" part of the whole thing, and that's where Poteto's pstack comes in.

    I just started using pstack, and I wanted to share my enthusiasm and how I've been bringing it into Wherewithal. That's the actual point of this post, even though I went on a huge story about how Everglae and Wherewithal came to be. I have this thing where I understand stuff better when I'm explaining it to someone, so this is my avenue for that.

    Feature maps

    The part of pstack I was most interested in is what Poteto calls the feature map. In her Complete Guide to pstack, she describes it as "an easily searchable map of all the features available in your app": what each one does and how a user gets to it. With a map like that, an agent can find its way around the app like a real user would, which makes it much better at checking its own work. The catch is that the map goes out of date as the app changes, so it has to be maintained.

    This clicked for me because, at this point, I was plugging in my Pixel before every release and getting Claude to go through everything again and again to make sure it all worked. This looked like a way to make that repeatable and structured.

    So I ran /pstack:create-verification-skill, which wrote a skill called verify-wherewithal. It lets a future agent boot the app, drive it like a user and save proof that a change actually works.

    How Claude drives the app

    Claude starts an Android emulator with no window, from a saved snapshot. On that snapshot the app is already set up with about two and a half months of made-up bank texts (115 of them) from three made-up banks, so there is real-looking data to poke at and none of it is mine. There is also a small script, sms.mjs, that sends a fresh fake bank text to the emulator whenever a journey needs one. It refuses to run against anything that isn't an emulator.

    To read the screen, Claude dumps the current UI as XML with uiautomator. Every button and label comes out as a node with its text, its accessibility label and where it sits on screen. Claude never picks coordinates itself. It asks a helper to tap something by its label, and the helper taps the centre of that node. Screenshots come from screencap and get saved outside the repo. And because the emulator boots with -no-snapshot-save, nothing a run does is kept, so every run starts clean and a journey can add or delete whatever it wants.

    Put together, one run looks roughly like this.

    One verification run
    Start Boot the snapshot, no window→ Open the app→ Health check
    Claude reads the feature map: a recipe for the journey a change touches
    follows it
    Read the screenas XML, every label
    Tap by labelnever coordinates
    Screenshotsaved as proof
    Check the mapdid the right thing show up?
    Repeat for
    every step
    over adb
    sms.mjs sends a
    fake bank text
    ↓
    Emulator no window 115 fake bank texts 3 made-up banks
    End Clean up. Nothing is saved, so the next run starts fresh

    One thing I got wrong at first: a normal run doesn't go through every flow in the app. It drives the feature a change touches. Going through everything is the maintenance pass's job (more on that below).

    Reading a feature map

    Alongside the skill's SKILL.md, it wrote the feature map itself: a folder of Markdown files, one per feature, plus a README that indexes them. Every feature file has the same four sections: the sub-features, how a user gets there, the exact commands to drive it along with what the screen should say after each one, and the gotchas that waste a run or fake a pass.

    I first thought of it as a test script, but it isn't really one. Nothing executes it. There's no assertion runner and no pixel diffing. It's a recipe that Claude reads and follows using its own judgement. I think that's why I find them hard to read, honestly. They're written for an agent, not for me. So I lined up one of them, the journey where a new bank text becomes a transaction, with what Claude actually does for each step.

    features/messages.md · what the map says vs what Claude does

      The bit I like most is the "second view". Claude doesn't count the screen saying "1 new transaction" as proof by itself. It goes and finds the transaction in another place, and then checks that Home's total went up by exactly the amount it sent. A 2,468 rupee text should take 138,298 to 140,766, and it did.

      Seeding and maintaining the map

      The first pass of /pstack:create-verification-skill only seeded six features (pstack's own advice is to start with your top three to five). Before handing the skill over, it also has to run it once end to end, which is why I could trust it on day one. Then I ran /pstack:maintain-verification-skill that same evening. It sent one subagent to read the source behind each feature, drove all of them live, added the three it missed (Home, Budgets and Subscriptions) and reworked the six it already had. A second pass the next day didn't add anything, just fixed recipes that had drifted from the app.

      The feature map, pass by pass

      Poteto recommends running the maintain pass at least once a day, ideally on a schedule. So far I have run it twice, by hand. That, and actually having lint and type checks run on every change, is probably where I go next.

      I also want to learn how to write my own lint rules, mainly so I can get rid of comments in the codebase. I'll admit this one is me blindly following advice from the podcast, but the way they explained why comments can suck made sense to me, so I want to try it out. And for the stuff that does need explaining, I heard that writing tests is a good way to lock that behaviour in instead. Loops and running a bunch of agents in parallel are way in the future, if this app ever takes off (if it even does).

I will try to keep these dev logs going, because writing them is how I get a deeper understanding of things, and it's also a nice way to share my progress and thoughts with you guys.
