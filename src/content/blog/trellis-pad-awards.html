I've always had a fascination with logos, but I'd never tried designing an actual logo or brand mark. Then I kept seeing posts promoting the Phoenix Arts and Design Awards, the PAD awards run by Phoenix Industries here in Sri Lanka. They had a graphic design category called Brands for Life, where you could compete by submitting a set of branding guidelines. I was interested. I also procrastinated a lot, so for a while I did nothing at all.

Coincidentally, that was around the time my feed started serving me reels of Allan Peters. He's a Minneapolis logo designer who runs Peters Design Company, has worked with brands like Nike, Amazon and Disney, and wrote the book Logos That Last. His videos went viral for walking through how a logo actually gets made, or fixing famous ones, and he uses a lot of physical stuff to show off his logos.

What makes him different is that he treats a logo as a process rather than a stroke of genius. He starts with the subject and the story, picks out the shapes that could work as building blocks, then combines them using things like negative space, overlaps and geometry. He pushes through a big pile of sketches and boils it down to about 15 options for the client. Watching him made me actually want to try logo design, and the PAD awards suddenly sounded like a good side quest.

The brief: revolution

The theme was revolution, and the idea was to come up with a brand and build an identity for it. The brand I came up with is Trellis, and it tries to revolutionize how students consume knowledge.

Instead of a yearly textbook that holds slices of many different domains, Trellis gives students a whole domain in a single book. When you have the entire domain in front of you, you can see why learning something matters by skipping ahead and seeing how everything links up. And if you forget something, you can catch up just by going back a level.

That ties in with the name. A trellis is an open framework of crossed bars, slats or wires, made of wood, metal, bamboo or plastic, that supports and guides climbing plants and vines. Knowledge gets something to climb.

I admit replacing school textbooks with books like this isn't a very practical or realistic idea. But I thought it was an okay foundation to build a brand on.

Winging it with triangles

I'd like to say I followed the Allan Peters approach to logo making, with the pile of sketches and the 15 options, but I didn't really. I remembered that bridge lattices are made of triangles, because a triangle is the one shape that holds rigid without bending out of form, so I kinda winged it with a design built from triangles.

I also wanted a logo that was flexible and could change forms. I liked the idea that it could fit into any space it gets thrown at and still be easily recognizable, even with multiple forms. So this is what I came up with.

Four triangles, four levels

If you look at the logomark, you'll notice the triangles that make it up are all different from each other. That's because Trellis takes a domain and divides it into four levels.

The first level is called Seed. It's introductory and entry level. The next is Shoot, where the knowledge from Seed is expanded further. Two more follow, Branch and then Canopy, which is the highest level. Each element in the logo stands for one of these levels, and each gets its own colour.

Counting corners

Look closer and you'll see each of the triangles has a shape cut out of it. One is a circle, the next is a line, the third is another triangle and the fourth is a trapezium. And they aren't random either. The cutouts represent their levels by their vertex count.

Seed is entry level, so its cutout has one vertex. Shoot's cutout has two, Branch's has three and Canopy's has four. The deeper you go, the more corners there are.

Strong idea, lacklustre execution

Overall I think the idea I came up with was really strong and intricate, but my execution of it was a bit lacklustre. I wish I had put more effort into making every part of the brand guidelines much better, and even introduced a custom typeface for Trellis, one that would really stand out and match the logomark.

But nevertheless, it got me a finalist spot at the PAD awards. Since this was my first time doing logo design and branding, I guess that wasn't a bad attempt at all.

If you want to see the real thing, my entry is in the PAD awards booklet among the special mentions for Brands for Life, and you can also go through the full submission I sent in.
